How it works

You meet the language before anyone explains it.

Patterns show up because you run into them five times in five situations, not because a lesson announced a rule. You are never asked to learn a rule before you use a sentence.

First

The scene comes first

A person, a place, a reason to speak. You hear natural Bengali, see the English, and understand before anything is explained.

Then

The help is taken away

You begin with English, roman Bengali, phonetics, and audio. One rung at a time those props come off, until the situation alone is enough.

After

Phrases come back

What you learn returns just before you would forget it, sometimes as a review, sometimes as a line from the person who first said it to you.

The scaffolding, and how it comes off

Every phrase climbs the same ladder.

Get it right and it moves up a rung. Miss it and it drops back down. A phrase only counts as yours once you have said it from memory, not just tapped through it.

Questions

The things people ask first.

Do I need to sign in?

Not to look around. The library is open to anyone: every word, every scene, the guides, the games, the word of the day. Signing in with Google only saves the story for you, where you are in the sixty days and which phrases you have produced. There is no email to confirm and no password to invent.

Do I have to learn the Bengali script?

No, and not to speak. Everything is taught in roman Bengali with a phonetic guide, so Amar cha chai rather than the letters. If you do want the script, there is a full writing lab that teaches it letter by letter, with a tracing pad. It sits beside the course, never in front of it.

What is this phonetics thing?

Two spellings for every word. The casual roman that learners actually write, and a phonetic line in the mouth of Kolkata, so you know that jol is said /dʒɔl/ and kemon achhen is /kæmon atʃʰen/. You hear it too, on every phrase.

Is this a grammar course?

No. You are never asked to learn a rule before you use a sentence. Register, whether you say tui, tumi or aapni, is taught as who you are talking to, not which table applies. The guides are there if you want the why, but you can ignore them and still finish the story.

How long is a session?

About ten to fifteen minutes. A scene, four or five new phrases climbing the ladder, and the full conversation to read and hear at the end. One chapter a day carries you through the arc.

Who made this, and why?

Someone with the same deadline as Kabir: a family, a language they did not share, and a real date on the calendar. Everything in the app exists because it was needed first. The thinking behind the syllabus is in the Writing section.
